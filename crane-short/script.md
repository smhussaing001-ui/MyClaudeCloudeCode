# How a Tree Branch Became a Tower Crane (Short, ~80s)

Each line below is one beat; the animation cuts on these beats.

1. **branch** — The simplest crane in the world is a rope slung over a tree branch. It works, right up until the branch breaks.
2. **jib** — So you swap the tree for a steel tower, and bolt a long beam across the top. That beam is called the jib.
3. **tip** — But slide a load out along the jib, and the whole thing wants to topple.
4. **counterweight** — So you add a short arm facing backwards, and pile it with twenty tons of concrete. That's the counterweight.
5. **climb** — Next problem: your building is catching up with your crane. So the crane climbs. Jacks raise everything above the tower, and a fresh six metre section slides into the gap.
6. **wind** — Keep going, and above eighty metres, the skinny tower flexes in the wind like a fishing rod.
7. **shaft** — So you bring the crane indoors, into the shaft meant for the lifts. Every few floors, it jacks itself up and braces against the concrete it just poured.
8. **stuck** — Then the skyscraper is finished, and you spot the flaw. The crane is on the roof, and nothing on the ground can reach it.
9. **derrick** — So it hoists up a smaller crane, piece by piece. The small one dismantles the big one, and lowers it to the street.
10. **smaller** — Now the small crane is stranded. So an even smaller one takes that apart. And on it goes, until the last crane is so tiny, it goes home in the lift.
11. **end** — Add a cab, a hook, and someone willing to climb up there at dawn. Congratulations. You've invented the tower crane.

## Fact notes
- Figures are typical values, not universal: counterweights commonly run 10-30 t, climbing sections are often 3-6 m, and free-standing height depends on the model (roughly 60-80 m before ties are needed).
- Internal climbing in a lift core, and dismantling with a roof derrick then progressively smaller cranes, are real practice.
