## A5 — Impact, One by One   (<= 700 words)

Ordered for a rising-stakes edit: how common it is → why she stays silent → no beds → the money → the deaths → whose deaths → the law that never came.

SEGMENT 1:
  DOMAIN: Prevalence — the treatment cascade
  HARD NUMBER: 30.8% identified → 15.8% treated → 6.3% adequately treated → 3.2% reach remission — 2016 systematic review, 32 studies, US postpartum-depression populations — [VERIFIED] — [S44]
  HUMAN TRANSLATION: Line up a hundred women who got depressed after having a baby. Thirty are noticed by anyone. Sixteen get some treatment. Six get enough of it. Three get well. The other ninety-seven carry it, most of them looking fine. (2016 review of older studies — not this year's data.)
  TRANSITION OUT: They fall out of the count because they never say a word — and there is a reason.

SEGMENT 2:
  DOMAIN: Culture — disclosure and the "good mother" script
  HARD NUMBER: Up to 50% of perinatal depression goes undiagnosed; mothers report fearing the screening questionnaire itself could trigger a child-protective-services report — US, 2024 review, corroborated 2016 and 2026 — [1-SOURCE] — [S91][S30][S90]
  HUMAN TRANSLATION: A nurse hands a new mother a ten-question form. Half the women who need help hand it back clean — they have done the math: tell the truth on this paper and someone might come for the baby. The tool built to find her is the thing she hides from.
  TRANSITION OUT: Say she does tell the truth, and she is sick enough to need a bed. Count the beds.

SEGMENT 3:
  DOMAIN: Infrastructure — inpatient capacity
  HARD NUMBER: 5 inpatient perinatal psychiatric units in the entire US (AR, CA, LA, NY, NC); the first opened at UNC in June 2011; none admit the baby overnight — PSI listing; peer-reviewed census 2022 — [1-SOURCE] — [S49][S50][S51]
  HUMAN TRANSLATION: 340 million people, 3.6 million births a year, five front doors for a mother in psychiatric crisis after childbirth. Britain — a fifth our size — runs twenty-two, and admits the baby with her. Here, if you are admitted, you leave the newborn behind.
  TRANSITION OUT: We never built the beds. That does not mean we stopped paying — we pay somewhere else.

SEGMENT 4:
  DOMAIN: Economics — the cost of not treating
  HARD NUMBER: $14.0 billion in modelled societal cost for the 2017 US birth cohort — about $31,800 per mother–child pair over six years — American Journal of Public Health, 2020 cost-of-illness model — [VERIFIED][PROJECTION] — [S59]
  HUMAN TRANSLATION: Every untreated case still sends its bill: ER visits, a job lost, a birth that comes too early, a child who needs more help later. About thirty-two thousand dollars per mother-and-child over six years — paid by all of us, just never spent on the care that would have worked.
  TRANSITION OUT: That is the money. Here is what the failure costs in people.

SEGMENT 5:
  DOMAIN: Mortality
  HARD NUMBER: Mental-health-condition pregnancy-related deaths judged preventable in 100% of cases (vs 64% for all other causes) — 14 US maternal mortality review committees, 421 deaths, 2008–2017; and by 2022 the #1 underlying cause, 216 deaths, 45 states — [VERIFIED] / [1-SOURCE] — [S36][S38]
  HUMAN TRANSLATION: Review boards read every pregnancy-related death file they could get. When the cause was mental illness, the verdict was the same every time: this one did not have to happen. Not most. Every one. And by 2022 this was the single leading cause of death for pregnant and postpartum women in America — two hundred and sixteen in one year.
  TRANSITION OUT: The boards also keep noting who dies, and who gets turned away first.

SEGMENT 6:
  DOMAIN: Equity
  HARD NUMBER: Mental health conditions are the dominant underlying cause of pregnancy-related death for American Indian and Alaska Native people — Child Trends analysis of CDC data, 2026 — [1-SOURCE] — [S40]
  HUMAN TRANSLATION: For Native mothers, the likeliest thing to end a life around childbirth is not hemorrhage and not the heart. It is a mental illness no one treated — the same gap as everywhere else in this story, widened, and handed to the women with the fewest clinics within driving distance.
  TRANSITION OUT: Every country writes its failures somewhere. Most wealthy ones wrote this one into law.

SEGMENT 7 — ACT-OUT:
  DOMAIN: Law
  HARD NUMBER: About two dozen countries have an infanticide statute recognizing postpartum mental illness (England & Wales since 1922/1938, routing such cases to mental-health commitment rather than prison); the United States has none — federally or in any state — and Illinois's 2018 law is only a sentencing mitigator, not a charge and not a defense — [1-SOURCE] — [S31][S72][S73][S74]
  HUMAN TRANSLATION: Two dozen countries looked at what a mind can do in the year after birth and decided the law had to account for it. The United States looked at the same thing and wrote nothing. One state, Illinois, since 2018, lets a judge weigh it — after the conviction is already in. Everywhere else in America, it is a fact the courtroom is not required to know.
  TRANSITION OUT: Cut to the courthouse — where that missing law is the empty chair.

ACT-OUT BEAT: Segment 7. The first six segments show a system underperforming — too few beds, misspent money, deaths ruled preventable. Segment 7 changes the category: America did not try and fall short, it never wrote the rule, while peer countries did it a century ago. "Gaps" become "a choice," and the viewer is handed straight into a trial where that missing law is the empty chair.
