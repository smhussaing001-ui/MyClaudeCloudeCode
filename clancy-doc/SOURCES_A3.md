# SOURCES — A3 (The Legal Crime: Origin Story)

New sources only. Numbering from S100. Format:
`[S##] Title — Outlet/Author, Date, URL — what it supports — reliability note`

[S100] "Deinstitutionalization Through Optimism: The Community Mental Health Act of 1963" — American Journal of Psychiatry Residents' Journal, 2021, https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp-rj.2021.160404 — CMHA signed Oct 1963; Congress authorized ~$150m over three fiscal years to build a national network of ~1,500 community mental health centers to receive discharged state-hospital patients; roughly half were ever built and sustained operating funds never followed. ⚠️ Search-summary only; peer-reviewed trainee journal of the APA. `[1-SOURCE]` on the "half built" figure.

[S101] "Deinstitutionalization of People with Mental Illness: Causes and Consequences" — AMA Journal of Ethics, Yohanna D, Oct 2013, https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/deinstitutionalization-people-mental-illness-causes-and-consequences/2013-10 — three drivers of deinstitutionalization: belief that asylums were inhumane, hope that new antipsychotics were curative, desire to cut cost; US state psychiatric hospital census peaked at 558,922 in 1955 and fell >90% over the following decades. ⚠️ Search-summary only; peer-reviewed ethics journal. `[VERIFIED]` on the 1955 peak (corroborates S102 indexing).

[S102] "Chlorpromazine and the Psychopharmacologic Revolution" — JAMA, 2014, https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/1844717 (also C&EN, https://cen.acs.org/articles/83/i25/Thorazine.html) — chlorpromazine marketed in the US as Thorazine and FDA-cleared in 1954 as the first widely used antipsychotic; it launched clinical psychopharmacology and gave overcrowded state hospitals a low-cost alternative to custodial care, enabling discharge. ⚠️ Search-summary only; JAMA historical review. `[VERIFIED]` on the 1954 date.

[S103] "Psychiatric Institutionalization and Deinstitutionalization" — Congressional Research Service, IF10870, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF10870 (mirror https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF10870.html) — between 1970 and 2018 the US closed roughly 84% of its state psychiatric hospital beds; community capacity did not expand to match. ⚠️ Search-summary only; CRS is a high-reliability federal research service. `[VERIFIED]` framing; open the PDF before broadcast.

[S104] "Mental Health Systems Act of 1980" / "Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981" — Wikipedia + ALA DttP, "The Mental Health Systems Act of 1980," Bell, https://journals.ala.org/index.php/dttp/article/view/7933 — the Mental Health Systems Act (signed by President Carter, Oct 1980) sought to renew federal leadership on community mental health; the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 (Reagan) repealed most of it within a year, folded remaining funds into state block grants, and federal mental-health spending fell. ⚠️ Wikipedia-level + library journal; **replace with the statutes / a history text before broadcast.** `[1-SOURCE]`.

[S105] "Payment for services in institutions for mental diseases (IMDs)" — MACPAC, https://www.macpac.gov/subtopic/payment-for-services-in-institutions-for-mental-diseases-imds/ ; "Medicaid's Institution for Mental Diseases (IMD) Exclusion" — CRS IF10222, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF10222 — the IMD exclusion has been in Medicaid statute since 1965; it bars federal Medicaid payment for most patients aged 21–64 in a psychiatric facility of more than 16 beds; it was written to keep the federal government out of funding large asylums and to leave inpatient psychiatric care a state responsibility. Public psychiatric beds are down more than 90–97% from their peak. ⚠️ Search-summary of MACPAC + CRS. `[VERIFIED]` on the 1965 statute and the 16-bed rule; `[1-SOURCE]` on the "97%" figure.

[S106] "Managing Managed Care" (Consumer Outcomes and Managed Behavioral Health Care) — National Academies / IOM, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK233216/ ; "Controlling inpatient psychiatric utilization through managed care" — PubMed 8610820 — through the 1980s and (accelerating) 1990s, employers and state Medicaid agencies moved psychiatric benefits into specialty "behavioral health carve-out" companies whose core mechanism is utilization review (preadmission review, continued-stay/concurrent review, case management); documented effect is shorter inpatient stays — e.g. the Massachusetts Medicaid carve-out cut length of stay for people with severe mental illness from 22.5 to 17.8 days in its first year, and overall from 9.7 to 8.7. ⚠️ Search-summary; the National Academies volume is authoritative, the LOS figures are from cited primary studies. `[1-SOURCE]` on the specific day counts.

[S107] "Peripartum and Postpartum Depression" — PsychDB, https://www.psychdb.com/mood/1-depression/postpartum-peripartum ; "Perinatal Depression" — StatPearls, NCBI Bookshelf NBK519070, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK519070/ — the DSM first acknowledged postpartum illness in DSM-IV (1994) as a "with postpartum onset" specifier, applicable only to onset within 4 weeks of delivery; DSM-5 (2013) renamed it "with peripartum onset" (pregnancy or within 4 weeks postpartum) after data showed ~50% of postpartum depressive episodes begin during pregnancy. It has never been a stand-alone diagnostic category. ⚠️ Search-summary; StatPearls is peer-reviewed reference. `[VERIFIED]` on the DSM-IV 1994 / DSM-5 2013 specifier history.

[S108] "From Infancy to Modern Day: The History of Mother and Baby Units in the United Kingdom" — PMC9378202, 2022 (same as bank [S53]) — confirms: joint psychiatric admission of mother and infant was pioneered by Thomas Main in 1948 at the Cassel Hospital, Surrey, England; initially for neuroses, later extended to severe mental illness, and adopted across the UK, Europe, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. ⚠️ Search-summary of the PMC abstract/text; peer-reviewed history. `[VERIFIED]` on the 1948 date / Main / Cassel — supersedes the "1940s–50s, verify" note on [S53].

[S109] "International Consortium of Women's Mental Health Experts Present Scientific Evidence to Support Classification of Postpartum Psychosis as a Distinct Disease" — Mount Sinai newsroom, 2025, https://www.mountsinai.org/about/newsroom/2025/international-consortium-of-womens-mental-health-experts-present-scientific-evidence-to-support-classification-of-postpartum-psychosis-as-a-distinct-disease ; "Make Postpartum Psychosis a Distinct Disorder in the DSM, Expert Panel Says" — Medscape, 2025, https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/make-postpartum-psychosis-distinct-disorder-dsm-expert-panel-2025a1000tue ; King's College London, https://www.kcl.ac.uk/news/postpartum-psychosis-should-be-recognised-as-a-distinct-disease — postpartum psychosis is not in the DSM-5 or the ICD; the only route to document it is a "perinatal/peripartum onset" specifier on another disorder (e.g. bipolar I, brief psychotic disorder). Downstream: no criteria to teach, treatment guidance rests on naturalistic cohorts and expert consensus rather than controlled trials, and the absence of a discrete code hampers awareness, insurance coding and dedicated research funding. An international consortium (incl. V. Bergink) has worked with the APA/DSM steering committee since 2020 to make it a distinct disorder. ⚠️ Search-summary of institutional press + trade coverage; the underlying is an expert-consensus paper, present as an argued position. `[1-SOURCE]`.
