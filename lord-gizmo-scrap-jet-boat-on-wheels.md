[02 · 00:37 · Foam + hot-wire cutter · 1:17 · 204 words]
(0:00–0:21 · marking foam, epoxy on scale · 58 words)
Right now this is just foam sheets, two old bicycles and some scrap metal. The NASAT Channel crew is turning it into a boat that drives across the beach on wheels and then heads into the river on a water jet they built themselves, with a few handy workshop tricks along the way. Welcome back to Lord Gizmo.

(0:21–0:50 · sheets joined, bricks on joints, outline traced · 80 words)
First they need one long panel for the hull. The sheet ends are cut at an angle so the glue has a bigger area to hold on to. The glue is epoxy, weighed out because it only cures properly with the right amount of hardener. Bricks keep the joints pressed down while it sets. For the outline, they bend a thin strip of wood across the foam and trace along it, an old boatbuilding method that gives a smooth curve.

(0:51–1:17 · building the hot-wire cutter · 66 words)
A saw would tear foam this thick apart, so they make a hot-wire cutter from two pieces of wood, a length of wire with a spring to keep it tight, and the welding machine to heat it. The current makes the wire hot enough to melt through foam. The spring is there because the wire gets longer as it heats up and would otherwise go slack.

[03 · 01:54 · Hull carving + fibreglass · 2:12 · 334 words]
(0:00–0:35 · two people carving the foam block · 92 words)
With the cutter ready, two people take one end each and guide the wire through the big foam block. They keep the pace slow and steady, since the wire needs time to melt its way through, and forcing it would make the wire bow and drift off the line. Little by little the block takes the shape of a hull, with offcuts piling up around them. Because the hull is solid foam, it will keep floating even if it gets damaged later on, which is a nice bonus for a homemade boat.

(0:36–1:04 · thinner layers sliced and glued, final trim · 69 words)
Next they slice some thinner layers of foam, resting the sheet on bricks so the wire can pass underneath. These layers are glued onto the hull with epoxy, and the bricks get used again as weights. After that, the wire goes over the hull once more to tidy up the shape and keep both sides even, because an uneven hull tends to pull to one side in the water.

(1:05–1:43 · fibreglass cloth + epoxy · 99 words)
At this stage the foam is still soft and would dent very easily, so the hull gets covered in fibreglass cloth. They roll epoxy into the cloth until it's soaked through, trying not to leave extra resin sitting on top, since that only adds weight. Using epoxy is important here. Polyester resin, which is cheaper and very common, reacts with polystyrene foam and melts it, while epoxy doesn't. Once it cures, the glass forms a hard skin over the light foam core. Surfboards are built in a similar way, and it makes the hull strong while keeping it light.

(1:44–2:12 · flipped on stools, grey coating, wooden edge strip · 74 words)
When the glass has cured, the hull is turned over onto two stools, smoothed down and given a grey coating. The coating also protects the epoxy, which slowly breaks down in sunlight if it's left bare. They glue a thin strip of wood along the top edge to protect it from knocks. It really looks like a boat now, but the next thing they do is cut a hole in the bottom of it.

[04 · 04:06 · Jet intake well + duct + nozzle · 2:36 · 365 words]
(0:00–0:23 · grinder cuts a rectangle through the hull bottom · 57 words)
There's a good reason for the hole. The boat is going to run on a water jet, and a jet pulls its water in from underneath the hull. So they mark out a rectangle near the back, cut through the fibreglass with a grinder and dig the foam out until they can see the ground through it.

(0:23–0:47 · plywood glued in, fibreglass mat + resin seals the opening · 56 words)
The problem now is all that bare foam around the edges of the cut. If it's left open, water will soak into it and the hull will slowly get heavier. So they glue plywood into the opening and cover it with fibreglass mat and resin, which lines the whole area and seals the foam in again.

(0:48–1:35 · PVC pipe + heat-bent plastic sheet + plywood flange = duct plug, sanded · 109 words)
Next they start on the intake duct, the curved channel that carries water from the bottom of the hull up to the pump. A piece of PVC pipe forms the round end. The rest is cut from a thin plastic sheet, warmed with a heat gun so it bends without cracking, and glued to the pipe, with a plywood ring added as a flange. The shape starts flat and rectangular and gradually turns round. That slow change lets the water flow smoothly into the pump, since a jet works best when the water arrives without much turbulence. Once it's together, they sand the joints so the outside is smooth.

(1:36–2:16 · release wax, glass over plug, grinder trim, plug chiselled out · 94 words)
What they've built so far is only a plug, a shape to make the real duct over. Resin sticks to almost anything, so first they rub on mould release wax. Then they lay fibreglass over the plug and wet it out with resin. When it has hardened, they trim the edges with a grinder and knock the plug out from the inside with a hammer and chisel. The plug gets broken up in the process, but it leaves behind a clean fibreglass duct in exactly the right shape, ready to fit into the hull.

(2:17–2:36 · plastic cup used as nozzle plug, cone ground to shape · 49 words)
The nozzle is made the same way, using a plastic cup as the plug. They glass over it, let it cure and grind off the ends to leave a cone. Squeezing the water out through a smaller opening makes it leave faster, and that's what pushes the boat along.

[05 · 06:42 · Stator + impeller + jet housing · 5:17 · 710 words]
(0:00–0:47 · lathe hub, stainless strips bent and welded · 108 words)
Over at the lathe, they turn down a short steel tube that will sit in the middle of the jet, with a hole through the centre for the shaft. Then they cut some small strips of stainless steel. Each strip is clamped in locking pliers and bent into a slight curve, and they tack them into place with a stick welder. It's slow, fiddly work, and every strip needs roughly the same bend so the water is treated evenly all the way around. Stainless is a sensible choice for this part, because it will spend its whole life in water and ordinary steel would rust away fairly quickly.

(0:48–1:42 · stator ring with vanes, plastic tail cone, welds ground · 118 words)
The curved strips are welded between the centre tube and an outer steel ring, and the result looks a bit like a small fan that doesn't spin. This part is called a stator, and it sits just behind the impeller. When the impeller pushes water backwards, it also sets the water swirling, and that swirl is energy going to waste. The fixed vanes of the stator straighten the flow out again, so more of the motor's effort goes into pushing the boat. Jet skis use the same idea. A pointed plastic cone is fitted on the back of the centre tube to help the water close up smoothly behind it, and then the welds are ground down neatly.

(1:43–2:33 · bearing housing on lathe, bearing pressed in, impeller on shaft with grub screw · 107 words)
Back on the lathe, they make a housing for a bearing and press the bearing into it. The housing is turned so the bearing fits tightly, which stops it from spinning around in its seat. The shaft will turn thousands of times a minute, so it needs to be held firmly in the centre, or the impeller would wobble and rub against the walls of the jet. The impeller itself has three wide blades. It slides onto a steel shaft and is locked in place with a small grub screw, tightened with an Allen key, so it can't slip on the shaft once the motor starts pulling.

(2:34–3:32 · duct trimmed, impeller fitted, housing glassed, drill test spin · 142 words)
Now they bring the pieces together. The fibreglass duct from earlier is trimmed with a jigsaw, and the impeller is fitted inside it on its shaft. More glass is wrapped around the round section to build a proper housing, with the stator sitting right behind the blades. The gap between the blade tips and the housing wall needs to be small, since any water that slips around the tips is water that doesn't get pushed out the back. The shaft also has to run straight through the middle, because a shaft sitting at an angle puts extra load on the bearing and wears it out early. Before anything is fixed into the boat, they put a drill on the end of the shaft and give it a test spin, which is a cheap way to check that nothing is catching or rubbing.

(3:33–4:12 · steel-rod intake grille, duct bonded into hull, outlet at stern · 98 words)
Across the intake opening they fit a row of steel rods, set into drilled holes and glued in place. Jets are well known for sucking up weeds, rope and plastic bags, and anything that wraps itself around the impeller will stop the boat. The rods keep the bigger rubbish out, and because they run along the direction the water flows, they don't slow it down very much. The duct then goes into the opening they cut in the hull, and it's bonded in with resin and glass, with the round outlet coming through the back of the boat.

(4:13–5:17 · flange drilled, stator glued, red gasket, housing bolted on, nozzle fitted · 137 words)
The last pieces are the stator housing and the nozzle. They drill bolt holes around the flange, glue the stator into its housing and add a red rubber gasket between the parts. The gasket seals the joint, since any leak here would let pressure escape before the water reaches the nozzle. Because the housing is bolted on, they can take the jet apart later if something gets jammed inside or a bearing needs replacing. With the impeller sitting inside the outlet, the stator housing goes on, the bolts are tightened, and the cone-shaped nozzle is fitted on the end. The whole water jet is now built into the boat, although none of it has been tested in the water yet. All it needs now is a motor to turn it, and that's what they work on next.

[06 · 11:58 · Pillow blocks + gearbox + motor + cooling coil + coupling · 3:31 · 456 words]
(0:00–0:19 · pillow block bearing on plywood, bracket welded · 40 words)
Inside the hull, they bolt a pillow block bearing onto a plywood base. It's a bearing in a sturdy cast housing with mounting holes, and it will hold the jet shaft straight. Then they weld up a small steel bracket.

(0:20–0:59 · right-angle gearbox stripped, washed, reassembled · 77 words)
Between the motor and the jet there's a right-angle gearbox, and this one looks like it has had a previous life. They take it apart and wash every piece in a basin, scrubbing out the old grease and dirt. It's worth the effort, because any grit left inside would grind away at the gear teeth every time it turns. Once it's clean, the housing is put back together and the screws go back in one by one.

(1:00–1:51 · brushless motor, adapter plate turned and drilled, bolted to gearbox · 107 words)
The motor is a brushless electric motor with three thick red leads. Brushless motors are small for the power they make, and they don't have brushes that wear out, which makes them popular for electric boats and bikes. To join the motor to the gearbox, they need an adapter plate. They turn it on the lathe and drill the mounting holes on a drill press. The plate has to hold the motor shaft exactly in line with the gearbox input, since even a small misalignment would put a side load on both sets of bearings. With the plate in place, the motor bolts straight onto the gearbox.

(1:52–2:35 · motor+gearbox unit, copper cooling coil, bracket, output shaft · 102 words)
Now it's one compact unit. The gearbox takes some of the motor's speed and turns it into extra turning force, and it also changes the direction of the drive by ninety degrees, which helps the whole thing fit inside a narrow hull. Next they wind copper tubing tightly around the body of the motor. Under a steady load a motor like this gets hot, and inside a closed hull that heat has nowhere to go. Water will later run through this coil to carry the heat away. They also weld a mounting bracket and fit a steel output shaft to the gearbox.

(2:36–3:31 · shaft in two pillow blocks, jaw coupling with yellow spider, heat shrink, motor mount welded · 130 words)
Down in the hull, the jet shaft now sits in two pillow block bearings. To connect the gearbox to the jet shaft, they machine a coupling on the lathe. It's a jaw coupling, with a yellow rubber insert shaped a bit like a flower sitting between the two metal halves. That rubber insert takes the shock when the motor starts up, and it also allows for a tiny amount of misalignment between the two shafts, which is very hard to avoid completely in a homemade boat. They slide heat shrink over the motor wires with a heat gun, then weld the motor mount into place so the whole drive is held firmly. With the water side finished, it's time to give this boat a way of getting around on land.

[07 · 15:29 · Rear bike drive + hinge mount · 4:30 · 560 words]
(0:00–0:33 · kids' bike cut down, rear wheel stripped · 81 words)
For the land side of things, they start with an ordinary blue twenty-inch kids' bike. Most of it isn't needed, so the grinder comes out and they start cutting away the parts they don't want. The rear wheel comes off and gets stripped down to the hub, because the standard bike gearing won't be much use once a motor is doing the work. It's a good example of how much of this build comes from things most people would throw away.

(0:34–1:07 · freewheel welded into big sprocket, fitted to wheel · 94 words)
They take a small freewheel, the part that normally screws onto a bike's rear hub, and weld it into the middle of a much bigger sprocket. That combination goes back onto the wheel. The big sprocket gives a lot of gear reduction, so a small sprocket on the motor can turn the wheel with plenty of force, which it will need to push a boat across soft sand. The freewheel also lets the wheel roll along on its own when the motor isn't pulling, instead of dragging the motor and gearbox around with it.

(1:08–1:47 · frame cut and welded, rear section hinged to hull, mounts glassed · 92 words)
Next they cut and weld the frame into a new shape. The rear part of the bike, with the wheel still attached, is going to hang off the back of the boat on a hinge. Steel arms are fitted to the frame and brought up to mounting points on the hull, and they lay fibreglass and resin over the mounts so the load is spread across a wider area. A bolt through a thin foam and glass skin on its own would tear out the first time the boat hit a bump.

(1:48–2:52 · second gearbox washed, red brushless motor bolted on · 97 words)
The wheel needs its own motor, and once again it comes with a gearbox that has to be cleaned first. Everything goes into the basin for a proper wash, and each piece is scrubbed before it's put back together. Keeping the old grease and grime out of it now saves a lot of wear later. The motor for this job is a red brushless motor. They bolt it onto the cleaned gearbox, line up the mounting plate and tighten everything down, checking that the motor shaft sits properly in the gearbox before the last screws go in.

(2:53–3:48 · drive sprocket bored on lathe, motor bracket welded, chain fitted · 115 words)
On the lathe they bore out a small sprocket so it fits the gearbox output shaft. The motor and gearbox are then mounted on a steel bracket welded to the bike frame, right beside the rear wheel, and a chain runs from the small sprocket down to the big one on the wheel. The two sprockets have to sit in a straight line with each other, otherwise the chain rubs on the teeth and wears out quickly. The chain also needs just a little slack, because a chain that's pulled tight puts extra strain on the bearings in the gearbox and the wheel. With the red motor clamped in place, the rear drive is finished.

(3:49–4:30 · small fittings welded, tab on frame, telescopic tube test-fitted · 81 words)
The last jobs are small ones. They weld nuts and threaded fittings onto short pieces of steel, add a mounting tab near the top of the bike frame, and then test fit a long telescopic tube between the frame and the hull. That tube is part of a system they build next. In the water the wheels would only create drag, so the team needs a way to lift them up and out of the way whenever the boat is afloat.

[08 · 19:59 · Wheel-lift actuators · 3:05 · 383 words]
(0:00–0:43 · thread cut on rod, brass nut, rod into tube · 96 words)
The way they lift the wheels starts on the lathe. They cut a long, coarse thread along a steel rod and machine a brass nut to run on it. When the rod turns, the nut is forced to move along the thread, so a spinning motion becomes a straight pushing or pulling motion. The rod goes inside a steel tube with the nut fixed to one end, and they grind and fit the ends so it all slides smoothly. Brass is a good choice for the nut, because it runs quietly against steel and wears slowly.

(0:44–1:07 · tab bonded to deck, arm fitted between hull and rear frame · 46 words)
A steel tab is welded up and bonded onto the deck with resin and glass, the same way as the hinge mounts earlier. That gives the lifting arm something solid to push against. Then the arm is fitted between the hull and the rear bike frame.

(1:08–1:27 · rear wheel lift test · 47 words)
Now they can check that it works. As the length of the arm changes, the rear frame swings on its hinge, and the wheel moves up and down behind the boat. It's a simple test, but it proves the idea before they build any more of them.

(1:28–2:38 · small geared motors stripped, cleaned, coupling machined, reassembled · 132 words)
The front wheels need lifting too, and for those they use a pair of small geared electric motors. They open one up, clean out the old grease and look over the gears. The housing is tidied up in the vice and with the grinder, and a coupling is machined on the lathe so the motor can turn a threaded rod. There's a useful side effect to using a screw thread this way. The weight of a wheel pressing on the nut can't easily turn the thread backwards, so the wheel stays where it's put even when the motor is switched off. Without that, the wheels could slowly drop back down while the boat is out on the water. Both motors are reassembled, and they check that everything turns freely before moving on.

(2:39–3:05 · motorcycle fork legs used as actuator bodies, motor fitted on end · 62 words)
For the body of each front actuator, they use the leg of an old motorcycle front fork. It's already strong and straight, and it's made to slide in and out. The geared motor goes on the end, with the threaded rod running down inside, and the last parts are finished on the lathe. Next, these actuators need some front wheels to lift.

[09 · 23:04 · Front wheels + double-wishbone suspension · 3:07 · 386 words]
(0:00–0:27 · flat-bar brackets drilled, ball joints (rod ends) · 57 words)
The front wheels get a much more car-like setup. They start with some flat steel bar, drilled and folded into small brackets, and a handful of ball joints, the same kind of rod ends you'd find on a car's steering. These little joints can swivel in almost any direction, which is exactly what a steerable wheel needs.

(0:28–1:19 · wheels serviced, disc rotors, knuckles with ball joints · 102 words)
Two bicycle wheels are stripped down to the hub, and the axles are cleaned and adjusted until the wheels spin freely. A disc brake rotor is bolted onto each one. Disc brakes make a lot of sense on this boat, because they keep working when they're wet, and these wheels are going to spend plenty of time in sand and water. Each axle then gets a small upright bracket, called a knuckle, with a ball joint at the top and another at the bottom. The wheel is carried on this knuckle, and the knuckle is what will turn when the boat steers.

(1:20–1:43 · lathe sleeves, bearings pressed in for pivots · 52 words)
On the lathe they cut short steel sleeves and press bearings into them. These become the pivots where the suspension arms will attach to the boat. Using proper bearings here means the arms can swing up and down smoothly, without the looseness you'd get from a bolt sitting in a plain hole.

(1:44–2:37 · tube arms bent in vice, pivots welded, upper/lower arms fitted · 111 words)
The arms themselves are made from steel tube. They bend each tube in the vice so it curves around the wheel, flatten and drill the ends, and weld the bearing sleeves onto the other side. Each wheel gets an upper arm and a lower arm, both joined to the knuckle through the ball joints. This is very close to the double wishbone suspension used on a lot of cars. With two arms guiding it, the wheel stays fairly upright as it moves up and down, so the tyre keeps good contact with the ground. They offer everything up to the wheel, check how it moves, and then weld the joints solid.

(2:38–3:06 · coil-over shocks fitted, two complete front assemblies · 64 words)
The final piece is a coil-over shock absorber, fitted between the arms. The spring soaks up bumps, and the damper inside stops the wheel from bouncing up and down afterwards. The whole front of the boat will rest on these two wheels, so they need to be tough. With both assemblies finished, disc brakes and all, it's time to attach them to the boat.

[10 · 26:10 · Assembly on hull: wheels, actuators, seat, cables, brakes · 4:18 · 511 words]
(0:00–0:37 · front assemblies bolted to bow, spirit level, mounts glassed, suspension checked · 91 words)
The two front wheel assemblies are bolted onto either side of the bow. Before anything is fixed for good, they put a spirit level on the deck to make sure the boat sits evenly, since a hull leaning to one side on land would put more weight on one wheel than the other. Where the mounts meet the hull, they grind back the paint and lay on glass and resin to spread the load. Then they push the wheels up and down by hand to check that the suspension moves freely.

(0:38–0:57 · fork-leg actuators connected to suspension arms · 50 words)
The fork-leg actuators from earlier are laid across the deck and connected to the suspension arms. When the motors wind the screws in, they'll pull the front wheels up towards the hull, and running them the other way will lower the wheels again when the boat comes back to shore.

(0:58–1:17 · green bike frame bonded into deck as seat and bars · 47 words)
Next comes somewhere to sit. They take the front half of a green bike frame, with the seat and the head tube still on it, and bond it straight into the deck with resin and glass. It becomes the seat and the handlebar mount in one piece.

(1:18–2:17 · steering arms welded, red-sheathed cables, adjusters, cable stops · 104 words)
Now they start on the controls. Small steel arms are welded onto the wheel knuckles so the wheels can be steered, and the cables start going in. Each cable runs inside a red outer sheath, and they fit threaded adjusters at the ends so the tension can be set properly. Cable stops are welded onto the frame to hold the sheaths in place. This matters more than it looks, because a cable only works if the outer sheath has something solid to push against. If the sheath can move, the lever just pulls the whole thing along and nothing happens at the other end.

(2:18–3:22 · cable guides, handlebars and levers, disc calipers fitted and adjusted · 117 words)
Under the green frame they weld small guides to keep the cables tidy and away from anything that moves. Then the handlebars go on, with brake levers fitted to them, and the cables are run down from the levers. Out at the front wheels, the disc brake calipers are bolted in place next to the rotors and the cables are connected, and the rear wheel gets its brake hooked up as well. They squeeze the levers, check that the pads grip the discs, and adjust the cables until both sides feel the same. Having both front brakes balanced is important, since a boat that brakes harder on one side will pull sharply that way when it stops.

(3:23–4:18 · blue cable to jet lever, steering linkage, bolts checked, actuator wires soldered · 102 words)
A blue cable is run all the way back to the stern, where it connects to a lever on the jet. Up at the front, they fit the linkage under the head tube that connects the handlebars to the steering. Everything is checked with a spanner, and loose bolts are tightened as they go. Finally, they solder the wires onto the actuator motors. Soldered joints hold up much better than twisted wires in a boat that's always damp and always shaking. It has almost everything it needs now, apart from paint and the rest of the electrics, and that's what comes next.

Before any paint goes on, they sand the intake grille and the area around it, so the paint has a rough surface to grip.

(0:48–1:05 · motor well painted, front suspension sprayed red · 43 words)
The motor well inside the hull gets a coat of dark paint too, which makes it easier to wipe out any water that gets in. The front suspension is taken off and sprayed bright red, which also protects the bare steel from rust.

(1:06–1:35 · red suspension and actuators refitted · 48 words)
With the paint dry, everything starts going back on. The red suspension is bolted back onto the bow, and the long actuators are laid across the deck and connected to the arms again. The jet motor is sitting in its freshly painted well, ready to be wired up.

(1:36–2:27 · brake cables, handlebars, rear jet linkage reconnected · 89 words)
The brake cables are fed back through their guides and reconnected to the calipers, and the tension is set again at each wheel. The handlebars go back on the green frame, with the levers and cables lined up so nothing gets pinched when the bars are turned. At the back of the boat, the linkage between the rear frame and the jet is refitted and adjusted. Putting everything back together takes a while, because each adjustment made earlier has to be checked again after the parts have been off.

(2:28–3:23 · soldering, heavy-duty plug and socket, heat shrink · 97 words)
Now for the electrics. The motor wires are soldered, and they use a heavy-duty plug and socket for the main power connection. A motor like this can pull a lot of current, and small connectors would heat up and could even melt. The big brass contacts in this plug can handle that, and it also means the power can be disconnected quickly, which is handy for charging and for safety. Every joint is covered with heat shrink tubing, warmed with a heat gun until it grips the wire, so the connection is insulated and sealed against damp.

(3:24–4:05 · plug to motor leads, speed controller, cooling hoses to copper coil · 80 words)
Down in the motor well, the plug is connected to the motor's three red leads. Those three wires go to a speed controller. A brushless motor can't run straight from a battery, since it needs a controller to switch the current between its three wires in the right order to keep it turning. Then the clear hoses are connected to the copper coil around the motor, so water from the jet can run through it and keep the motor cool.

(4:06–4:29 · red toggle switches for actuators · 47 words)
For the wheel actuators, they wire up a set of red toggle switches and mount them on the frame near the handlebars. Flipping a switch reverses the current to a motor, and that's what makes it run one way or the other, raising or lowering a wheel.

(4:30–5:35 · wires tied along deck and frame, walk-around · 116 words)
The wires are run along the deck and up the frame, and they're tied down with cable ties all the way. That's important on something with moving suspension, because a loose wire can easily get caught and pulled apart as the wheels move. Seen from the side, the boat is really coming together now. There's the grey hull with its red stripe, the red front suspension, the green frame in the middle and the blue rear frame at the back, all connected by cables and wires. At the stern, the rear frame sits just above the jet outlet, and the front wheels sit out to the sides of the bow where they won't touch the hull.

(5:36–5:59 · control board, thumb throttle, metal electrics box · 50 words)
A small control board is connected into the system, along with a thumb throttle like the ones used on electric bikes. Pressing the throttle sends a signal through to the speed controller, and the motor speeds up to match. The electrics are closed into a metal box inside the hull.

(6:00–6:41 · rear drive, nozzle, jet motor with hoses · 79 words)
They check over the rear drive, with the red motor sitting beside the rear wheel and the actuator arm running forward from the frame. From behind, you can see straight into the nozzle of the jet. Inside the hull, the jet motor now has its cooling hoses connected and its actuator arm sitting just above it. Every part they have built so far is finally in place, and they give the whole thing one last look before testing it.

(6:42–7:33 · rider sits on, steering check, rear wheel lift test · 91 words)
The first real test happens right there in the yard. One of the team sits on the seat, and you can see the suspension settle a little under the rider's weight, which is exactly what it should do. They turn the handlebars to check the front wheels steer together. Then they try the actuators, and the rear wheel swings up behind the boat and comes back down again. Everything seems to work on dry ground, so next they take it down to the beach to see how it copes with sand.
