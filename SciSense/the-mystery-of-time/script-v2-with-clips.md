# THE MYSTERY OF TIME: Script v2 (real clips, licensed sources)

This replaces the **SCRIPT** section of `script-and-production-plan.md`. The Visual Sources, Motion Graphics and Fairlight sections in that file still apply.

**Runtime:** ~11 min (narration ~7 min + 8 clips ≈ 4 min)
**Voices:** Narrator (spine) · Stephen Hawking · Brian Greene · Sean Carroll · Neil deGrasse Tyson · Ann Druyan

**How the blend stays coherent:**
- Every clip is *set up* by the line before it and *paid off* by the line after it.
- Act 1 asks a question ("the laws don't care which way time runs, so why do we?"). Sean Carroll answers it in Act 5.
- Hawking frames the whole film: his party opens it, his voice closes it.

## SCRIPT

Legend: **N:** narration · **▶ C#** clip from the sheet · **[V]** visual · **[MG]** motion graphic · **[SFX]/[MUS]** sound · **[LT]** lower-third caption

---

### COLD OPEN (0:00–0:55)

[V] Black. [SFX] One clock tick, heavy reverb. Slow push into the Hubble Ultra Deep Field.

**N:** On June 28th, 2009, Stephen Hawking threw a party at the University of Cambridge.
There was champagne. There were balloons. A banner read: *Welcome, Time Travellers.*
But Hawking sent out the invitations only *after* the party had ended.
He waited.
No one came.

[SFX] Tick… silence.

**N:** It was a joke, and an experiment. If travel into the past were possible, someone, someday, would read that invitation… and come back.
So why didn't they?
To answer that, we have to ask a question that sounds simple, and has defeated every great mind who has tried it.
What… is time?

[SFX] Sub-boom + reverse swell. [MG] **THE MYSTERY OF TIME**

---

### ACT 1: THE THING WE CAN'T DEFINE (0:55–2:05)

[V] ISS timelapse: Earth's day/night line, city lights.

**N:** You measure it every day. You run out of it. You waste it, save it, kill it.
And yet physics has no agreed answer to what it actually is.
We feel time flowing, carrying us from past, to present, to future. Always forward. Never back.

[MG] Planet orbit plays forward, then smoothly in reverse.

**N:** But here's the strange part. Almost every fundamental law of physics works exactly the same whether time runs forwards… or backwards.
Play a film of planets orbiting in reverse, and nothing looks wrong. The equations don't care.
*So why do we?*
Hold on to that question. We'll come back to it.

**N:** For most of human history, we were certain of one thing: time is the same for everyone. A second here is a second everywhere.
In 1905, a 26-year-old patent clerk proved that wrong. And more than a century later, it still astonishes the people who study it.

▶ **C1, Brian Greene** [LT: BRIAN GREENE · Theoretical Physicist, Columbia University]
> "I first learned about special relativity about 35 years ago, but even today it still blows my mind. I mean, Einstein discovered that space and time have weird, unexpected features. Time can slow down or speed up. Space can stretch or compress…"

---

### ACT 2: EINSTEIN BREAKS THE CLOCK (2:05–3:25)

[V] Public-domain Einstein photo, slow push-in.

**N:** Einstein realised that the speed of light is the same for every observer, no matter how fast they're moving.
And if light's speed can't change… something else has to.
That something is time.

[MG] **Light clock.** [SFX] The tick motif slows down in sync.

**N:** The faster you move through space, the slower you move through time.
It sounds like philosophy. It isn't. It's been measured.
In 1971, physicists flew atomic clocks around the world on commercial jets. When they landed, the clocks no longer agreed with identical clocks left on the ground, just as Einstein predicted.
High above us, cosmic rays create particles called muons. They live for barely two millionths of a second, far too short to reach the ground. And yet we detect them down here, constantly. Their clocks, from our point of view, are running slow.
Time dilation isn't a theory. It's a fact of nature.

---

### ACT 3: GRAVITY BENDS TIME (3:25–4:55)

[MG] Spacetime grid; Earth sinks into it.

**N:** Ten years later, Einstein went further. Gravity, he said, isn't really a force. It's the shape of space and time itself, bent by mass.
The world needed proof. It came from the shadow of the Moon.

[V] NASA total solar eclipse footage.

▶ **C2, Brian Greene**
> "1919 was the year that observations of distant stars, made visible by the solar eclipse of 1919, allowed Einstein's general theory of relativity to undergo its first rigorous test, because Einstein's theory says that space and time can warp and curve, which would mean that distant starlight would curve as it passes by the sun."

**N:** The starlight bent. Einstein was right. And where spacetime bends, time slows down.
Your head is ageing slightly faster than your feet. Scientists have measured this with atomic clocks just thirty-three centimetres apart.

[V] GPS satellite render. [MG] "Ground" vs "Orbit" clocks, "+38 µs / day".

**N:** GPS satellites orbit about twenty thousand kilometres up, where gravity is weaker. Their clocks run fast by about thirty-eight microseconds a day. Without Einstein's correction, your phone's map would drift by around ten kilometres every day.
Now take that to the extreme.

[V] NASA SVS black hole visualization. [MUS] Music drops out; drone only.

**N:** Near a black hole, gravity is so intense that time almost stops.
To a distant observer, anything falling in seems to slow… freeze… and fade at the edge.
But for the one falling in, nothing feels strange at all. Their watch ticks normally… right up to the point of no return.

---

### ACT 4: PAST, PRESENT AND FUTURE AT ONCE (4:55–6:10)

*(Narrator-only act. This deliberate breather from clips keeps the narrator as the backbone.)*

[V] Webb deep field, very slow zoom.

**N:** If time can stretch and slow, something even stranger follows.
There is no universal *now*.
Two observers moving differently will disagree about which events are happening at the same moment, and neither of them is wrong.

[MG] **Spacetime loaf:** a block of frames sliced at two different angles = two different "nows".

**N:** Some physicists take this to its logical end. They picture the universe as a single four-dimensional block, with every moment, past, present and future, laid out together like frames on a film reel.
In this block universe, the past hasn't vanished. The future isn't unwritten. They simply… *are*.

[V] Earth at night from orbit. [MUS] Soft piano.

**N:** Einstein seemed to find comfort in this. When his oldest friend, Michele Besso, died in 1955, Einstein wrote to Besso's family that, for physicists like them, the distinction between past, present and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion.
Weeks later, Einstein himself was gone.

---

### ACT 5: WHY TIME HAS A DIRECTION (6:10–7:20)

[SFX] Tick motif **in reverse**.

**N:** Which brings us back to our question.
If the laws of physics don't care which way time runs… why do we?
Why do we remember yesterday, but not tomorrow? Why do eggs break, but never un-break?

[MG] **Entropy box:** hot (red) and cold (blue) particles mix into uniform purple and never separate again.

▶ **C3, Sean Carroll** [LT: SEAN CARROLL · Theoretical Physicist]
> "…irreversibility, my favorite feature of the macroscopic world. Certain things happen in one direction of time but never the opposite direction. Bring a hot thing and a cold thing together into contact, they will both become warmer. Bring two warm things into contact, they will never separate and become hot and cold. That is a true feature of thermodynamics known as the second law of thermodynamics, and it has absolutely no analog in the laws of molecules and atoms. The laws of atoms are completely reversible. There is no arrow of time at the level of atoms. That does not mean that the arrow of time that we observe in our world is not real…"

**N:** And that same one-way arrow explains something even more personal: why we remember yesterday, but never tomorrow.

▶ **C3b, Carlo Rovelli** *(optional)* [LT: CARLO ROVELLI · Theoretical Physicist]
> "We know the past, the past is fixed. Why is the past fixed? Because we know it. Why do we know it? Because we have traces of it. I have pictures of myself as a young kid. There are fossils, steps in the sand that indicate to me that somebody walked in the past on the sand, not in the future. The craters on the Moon refer to the fact that a stone fell down. There's nothing of the sort related to the future."

*Audio note: this was an online (Zoom) colloquium, so expect laptop-mic quality. Use heavier Voice Isolation, or drop the clip if it doesn't match. Start at 26:33, **not** 26:00; at 26:00 he misspeaks ("we remember the future").*

**N:** That one-way slide, from order to disorder, is called entropy. And it *is* the arrow of time.
Follow that arrow backwards, and it points to a single moment, 13.8 billion years ago, when the universe was astonishingly ordered.
Every tick of every clock since then is the universe slowly unwinding from that beginning.

---

### ACT 6: DID TIME BEGIN? (7:20–8:30)

[V] Planck cosmic microwave background map. [MG] Timeline zooms from "Today" back to "13.8 billion years ago".

**N:** Which raises the most unsettling question of all. If time has a direction… did it have a *start*?
For centuries, people imagined time as a stage that had always been there, stretching forever in both directions. Einstein's theory changed that, and no one understood the consequences better than the man who threw the party.

▶ **C4, Stephen Hawking** [LT: STEPHEN HAWKING · 2007]
> "…general relativity predicted that the universe and time itself would begin in the Big Bang. It also predicted that time will come to an end in black holes."

**N:** So what came *before* the Big Bang?

▶ **C5, Stephen Hawking**
> "However, in 1915, Einstein introduced his revolutionary general theory of relativity. In this, space and time were no longer absolute, no longer a fixed background to events. Instead, they were dynamical quantities that were shaped by the matter and energy in the universe. They were defined only within the universe, so it made no sense to talk of a time before the universe began. That would be like asking for a point south of the South Pole."

[V] Slow aerial of Antarctic ice (NASA Operation IceBridge footage) → fade to stars.

**N:** No before. No outside. Time isn't the stage the universe stands on. It's part of the universe itself.

---

### ACT 7: CAN WE TRAVEL THROUGH TIME? (8:30–9:40)

[V] ISS exterior; EVA footage.

**N:** So… can we travel through time?
Into the future: yes. We already have.
Cosmonaut Sergei Krikalev spent more than eight hundred days orbiting Earth. By the time he came home for good, he had travelled about one-fiftieth of a second into the future.
Go somewhere gravity is stronger, and the effect is far more dramatic. Brian Greene once turned this into a modern myth, a retelling of Icarus, where a boy ignores his father's warning and flies his ship not towards the Sun… but to a black hole.

[V] Black hole visualization again, slower. [MUS] Tension rising.

▶ **C6, Brian Greene**
> "The boy goes to a black hole, hangs out there for just a few hours, but the real physics that Einstein taught us is that time slows down near the edge of a black hole. So even though it was just a few hours for him, when he comes back to show his dad what he's done, he quickly learns that it's 10,000 years into the future, and his dad has been dead for 10,000 years."

[SFX] Single low tick.

**N:** A one-way ticket to the future. Physics allows it.
The *past* is a different story.
Einstein's equations do permit exotic shapes of spacetime that loop back on themselves: wormholes, paths that return to their own beginning. But every one demands something we have never found, like matter with negative energy. And every one opens the door to paradox.
What happens if you go back… and stop yourself from ever leaving?

[MG] Wormhole fold.

**N:** Hawking suspected nature forbids it. He called it the Chronology Protection Conjecture: the idea that the laws of physics conspire to keep the universe safe for historians.
And his party remains some of the best evidence we have.
No one came.

---

### CLOSING (9:40–11:00)

[V] Night sky timelapse; slow push into Andromeda.

**N:** Tonight, look up.
The light from the nearest stars left them years ago. The faint smudge of Andromeda left it two and a half million years ago.
You never see the universe as it is. Only as it was. Every glance at the sky is a glance into the past.
And yet, the atoms in your body were forged in those same stars.

▶ **C7, Neil deGrasse Tyson** [LT: NEIL DEGRASSE TYSON · Astrophysicist]
> "You can celebrate the fact that my molecules are up there, and that we all came from stars. There's a sense of participation and belonging that is almost spiritual in the feelings it elicits."

**N:** Thirteen point eight billion years have passed since time began. It sounds like an eternity. It may be only the beginning.

▶ **C8, Ann Druyan** [LT: ANN DRUYAN · Co-writer, *Cosmos*]
> "We're just beginning to find our way. We're very young as a species, but the universe is very young too, and it has a very much longer future than the time that has passed, the 13.8 billion years that have passed, and so the possibilities are virtually infinite."

[V] Pull back: Earth → Solar System → galaxy.

**N:** Maybe time is a river. Maybe it's a block. Maybe, at the deepest level, it doesn't exist at all.
Somewhere out there, Hawking's invitation is still waiting.
And he never stopped believing we'd figure it out. We just need… time.

▶ **C9, Stephen Hawking**
> "If I'm right, the universe is self-contained and governed by science alone. In time, we can hope to understand it completely. We have long enough, as the universe should last forever. Eternity is a very long time, especially towards the end, as Woody Allen said."

[SFX] Final clock tick. Cut to black.

[MG] End card: **SciSense: the universe is stranger than you were taught.**

## SOURCING NOTES

- **Brian Cox (physicist):** I found **no genuinely CC-licensed talk of him discussing time** from an official uploader. Every "Creative Commons" Cox video on YouTube about relativity or entropy is a re-upload (e.g., "Ultimate Documentary HD"), and a re-uploader can't license what they don't own. The British Library event is CC BY, but Ann Druyan does most of the talking, which is why she's in the closing instead. The "Brian Cox in Conversation" video from the Scottish Parliament is the *actor* Brian Cox.
- **Michio Kaku and Kip Thorne:** no official CC uploads of either on time travel. ICTP's Kip Thorne uploads are CC BY but technical, and their transcripts were blocked by YouTube rate-limits during research. Worth a second look if you want a wormhole voice in Act 7.
- **Why these are safer, not 100% safe:** CC BY from the original institution is the strongest position available, but Content ID can still mis-fire. If you get a claim, dispute it citing the CC BY licence and the source URL. Keep the description credits exactly as above.
- **Rules followed in the cut list:** each clip is a continuous passage in the speaker's own words, with no splicing of separate sentences into a new meaning.
