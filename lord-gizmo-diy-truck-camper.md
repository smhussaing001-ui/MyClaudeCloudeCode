One Man Builds A Camper On A Truck — Alone, In 30 Days
Title: 自作キャンピングカー総集編です！ (Homemade Camper — The Complete Build) | いしころちゃんねる / Ishikoro Channel

---

Welcome back to another video for Lord Gizmo.

It is amazing how some people can look at an empty truck bed, see a house, and then just go ahead and build one. That is exactly what the builder behind Ishikoro Channel does here. No crew, no workshop team, no help — from the first weld to the last coat of paint, he does the whole thing himself. And by his own count, it takes about thirty days of eight hour shifts. Let's follow it from the ground up.

It begins with steel. The base frame is cut from fifty by fifty square pipe with a two point three millimetre wall, chopped to length on an abrasive saw in a shower of sparks. He works flat on the workshop floor, laying the members out, clamping them, and using lengths of timber as squaring jigs so every corner is held true before it is tacked. That step is worth pausing on. A base frame that goes together a couple of millimetres out of square never comes back — every wall, every window and every door built on top of it inherits the error, and you do not find out until the last panel refuses to fit.

Once the shape is held, the tacks are turned into full welds and the exposed ends are ground back and rounded off. The uprights go on next, then a curved arch at the rear, and suddenly the flat ladder on the floor becomes a three dimensional cage the size of a small room. It is honest work rather than showroom work — he says himself it is his first welding in a few years — but it is square, it is braced, and it is strong enough to carry a house down a motorway.

With the steel done, attention shifts to the floor. Sheets of forty millimetre extruded polystyrene are cut to drop between the frame members, and twenty four millimetre plywood goes down over the top. That is a serious thickness of decking, and it earns its keep twice — it stiffens the whole platform against twisting, and it gives him something solid to screw into for the rest of the build. The insulation underneath matters just as much, because the floor of a truck camper sits in moving air all day. Skip it, and in winter that deck becomes a cold plate you cannot heat your way out of.

Now the shape of the thing appears — and it appears from the top down. Before a single wall stud goes in, straight laminated rafters are laid across the top of the steel frame and fixed down one after another. It looks like an odd order until you see what it buys him: those rafters tie the tops of the steel posts together and stop them spreading, so the frame is locked square before any of the timber work begins. Only then do the wall studs rise between the steel uprights. This hybrid of steel and timber is a smart bit of thinking — the steel takes the punishment of the road, while the timber gives him fixing points everywhere he needs them and does not conduct cold straight through the wall the way a full steel cage would.

Inside the shell, the layout gets framed out — a tall cubicle for the closet and toilet, carcasses for the kitchen units, and extra steel uprights welded in around the door opening where the loads concentrate. Then insulation goes in properly: blue polystyrene cut to fit and pressed into every single stud bay. Insulation is only ever as good as its worst gap, so a wall that is ninety percent insulated is not ninety percent warm — the cold simply finds the ten percent you missed and pours through it.

Over the outside goes a waterproof, breathable membrane, wrapped around the entire shell and taped up. The order here is not optional. Wiring and insulation go in first, then the membrane, then the cladding — get it wrong and you are pulling finished work back off to fix it. The membrane sheds rain from the outside while still letting moisture vapour escape from within, which is the difference between a wall that dries out and a wall that quietly rots behind the timber.

The windows are next, and this is where he really commits. He builds the timber frames himself on the bench, then fits proper aluminium sashes, complete with insect screens, sealing and taping around every opening. Here is the detail that tells you how seriously he took this: those windows cost ninety three thousand yen — more than the steel frame, the cedar cladding and the entire roof combined. It is not extravagance. Openings are where a build like this leaks, whistles and works itself loose, and cheap windows in a vehicle that vibrates for hours at a time will find you out.

With the shell weathertight, the inside begins. Plywood goes up first, then pine tongue and groove lining boards across the walls and over the curved ceiling, glued and pinned board by board with a brad nailer. Curved trims are cut and shaped around the windows so the panelling flows into the openings rather than stopping dead at them. It is slow, fiddly work, and all of it is done solo — holding a full length board in place with one hand while nailing with the other is exactly as awkward as it looks.

Meanwhile the joinery piles up. Laminated pine gets cut into worktops, a bench and a table. A white panel is cut for behind the kitchen, which on a timber lined box with a gas hob in it is not a decorative choice — it is fire protection. Doors are built from scratch, including a glazed one, and later the entrance door gets its lock mortised in entirely by hand, chiselled out of the stile with a mallet and a sharp edge. There is no undo on that one. Cut the mortise a few millimetres too deep or too wide and you are making a new door.

The upper walls are finished in wallpaper, taped and caulked neatly into the corners against the timber, and a wood effect cushion floor is rolled out and bonded down across the deck. An overhead cabinet goes in, and a length of LED tube is hidden inside a purpose built box so the light washes up across the ceiling instead of glaring down at you. Small touch, big difference — indirect lighting is what stops a compact space feeling like the inside of a van.

Up top, the roof gets its own treatment. Glass wool insulation is packed between the rafters — the roof is the single biggest heat gain in a parked vehicle sitting in the sun, so this is the one to get right — and plywood goes down over them to form the deck. It runs flat, with one exception: the leading edge above the cab is rounded off, and there the plywood has to be bent around the curve and held while it takes the shape. That rounded nose is not decoration. It is the edge that meets the air first at motorway speed, where a square corner would drum, whistle and drag. Once the deck is on, he snips black edge trim to length by hand, runs thick beads of adhesive across the plywood, and bonds the sheet metal roof straight down onto it. No nails, no screws. That sounds like a gamble until you remember what a fastener actually is on a roof: a hole. Every one of them is a future leak working itself loose under vibration, and this thing is going to spend its life being shaken down the road. Bonded across its whole surface, that sheet is not going anywhere.

The exterior is cedar board, fixed with brass nails, and then painted — three coats of Xyladecor, which soaks into the timber rather than sitting on it, so it protects against sun and rain without flaking off in a year. The natural cedar disappears under a deep charcoal, and the whole cabin suddenly looks less like a project and more like a piece of architecture. Then the air conditioner goes in, mounting plate screwed to the wall and a core drilled through for the pipework. This is the one part of the build he is not learning on the fly — air conditioning and electrical work are his actual trade.

And then comes the moment of truth. The finished cabin is walked out of the workshop, slung from a truck mounted crane, and set down on timber trestles. Everything welded weeks ago — every hook, every eye nut, every corner of that square frame — gets tested in one lift. It holds. The Fuso Canter reverses underneath, the trestles come out, and the house he built on the floor of his workshop settles onto the truck it was always meant for.

What comes out the other side is genuinely lovely. The charcoal exterior with its pale timber door surround looks deliberate and expensive. Inside, pine lines the lower walls and the ceiling, coved softly where the two meet, warm cream wallpaper lifts the upper half, and the light from that hidden LED strip washes softly across the roof. There is a stainless sink and a two burner hob set into a laminated pine worktop, storage benches with lift up lids, curtains at the windows, a ventilation fan you can turn up or down, air conditioning, and a full width bunk tucked into the nose over the cab. It is compact, but nothing about it feels cramped or improvised — it feels like a small house that happens to have a truck underneath it.

And then there is the bill, laid out honestly at the end. Four hundred and seventy thousand yen in materials. Three hundred and forty four thousand in equipment — and the biggest single line there is the portable power station at a hundred and sixty thousand. Five hundred and forty thousand for the truck itself, bought at auction with a valid inspection. One million three hundred and sixty four thousand yen all in, and he reckons the true figure is probably about fifty thousand higher than that. For a fully insulated, air conditioned, plumbed and wired home on wheels, built by one man in about thirty working days, that is remarkable.
